## 1. The thesis in one breath

> **Markets deliver technology to people as customers. Nothing delivers the ability to build with it. A few people find that ability through hobbies that spread friend to friend. Most never find out it exists. Schools can reach everyone else, if we back the teacher and let students share sideways.**

*Revised Oct 2, 2026. The first version ("the second gap has no market; it has a teacher") overclaimed. See §2.8 for the counterarguments it had to survive.*

Four supporting claims. The talk is these four plus a question to the room.

1. **Gap 1 has investors and public money.** Turning research into products is pulled closed by capital *and* by governments: defense, NIH, NSF, DARPA. The "valley of death" exists because markets alone won't pay for early work. Either way, money is pointed at it.
2. **Gap 2 is a capability gap, not a product gap.** Markets got drones into millions of hands as products. What they don't deliver is the ability to build, fix and point the technology at a local problem, or uses whose value goes to the public (a runoff map). **Some people already know what's possible** and find it themselves. **Most don't know they're customers yet.** You can't ask for a runoff survey if you don't know a drone can do one in an afternoon.
3. **Knowledge moves vertically in institutions and laterally in hobbies.** In a company, one team solves a problem, sends it up the chain, and it comes back down to another team months later, if at all. School works the same way: teacher to student, top down. Hobbies are different. Sports, games and FPV drones spread sideways: one kid learns a trick and the whole lunch table has it by Friday. Lateral spread is faster, but it only reaches people already in the hobby. **Schools reach everyone but move knowledge vertically. The program has to build the lateral channel in** (§2.9).
4. **Getting it to the many takes specialist go-betweens.** A musician with a great record doesn't reach the masses alone: a **producer** makes it something people can listen to, and a **promoter** books the venues and gets people in the door. Technology is the same. Researchers, engineers and hobbyists make it. Someone has to package it into a kit and a lesson that work in 45 minutes (the producer: Drone Edge's role). Someone has to put it in front of a community (the promoter and venue: the teacher, the school, the showcase, the competition).

The question to the room: *your incentive closes gap 1. What closes gap 2, and will you help?*

**What history adds (§2.7):** every deliberate attempt to close gap 2 shows the same thing. **The device is never the bottleneck. The teacher and the town are.**

## 2. The framework, in more depth

Use this section for Q&A prep. Don't deliver it as a lecture.

### 2.1 Gap 1: research → engineering

| What closes it | Example the room will recognize |
|---|---|
| Venture capital, corporate R&D | Every startup built on a paper |
| Defense and government programs (SBIR, DARPA-style funding) | **This weekend's theme.** C-UAS exists as a hackathon because someone will buy it. |
| University tech transfer (Bayh-Dole Act, 1980) | Universities may own and license federally funded inventions. That was a policy choice made specifically to close gap 1. |
| Open source + hiring | Publish the stack, get hired for knowing it |

**Point to land:** gap 1 closes because someone captures the value. Policy has even been written to make that capture easier.

### 2.2 Gap 2: engineering → communities

Why nothing pulls it closed:

| Force | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Diffuse value** | A runoff map helps the township, the homeowners downstream and the creek. None of them pays for it alone, so it's a public good. |
| **Fragmented, small buyers** | Thousands of districts and townships, each with its own procurement, board cycle and budget year. The cost of selling exceeds the deal size. |
| **Expertise cliff** | The hardware is cheap now, but the know-how to use it safely and legally (Part 107, airspace, data handling) is not. |
| **Awareness** | The deepest one. Markets respond to demand, and demand needs awareness first. Most people don't know what a sub-$300 aircraft that flies itself can do for their street. Unknown unknowns don't show up as purchase orders. |

**Point to land:** gap 2 isn't a technical problem, and it isn't really a cost problem anymore either. It's a distribution and awareness problem. Nobody is paid to solve it.

### 2.3 This has been solved before, once, on purpose

**Agricultural Cooperative Extension (Smith-Lever Act, 1914).** The US built a deliberate bridge from land-grant university research to farms: a county agent in every county, funded publicly, whose job was gap 2.

Part of how it spread was **youth clubs, the roots of 4-H.** Young people were more willing to try new methods than their parents. A kid grew the better seed corn, the yield was visible at the county fair, and adults adopted it. **The student was the awareness channel.**

> We don't have a county agent for drones, sensors or robotics. But we do have a high school in every county.

(Verify the history phrasing against the USDA NIFA and National 4-H histories before delivery. The broad outline is well documented, but quote it carefully.)

### 2.4 Why schools specifically

- **Present everywhere.** Even towns without a library branch, a makerspace or a university have a school.
- **Trusted channel into every household.** A newsletter, a showcase night or a kid at the dinner table reaches families no ad reaches.
- **Already funded to teach careers.** CTE programs, state CTE money and grants already exist for this; see [`../../drone/src/app/schools/funding/page.tsx`](../../drone/src/app/schools/funding/page.tsx). Don't name a funding stream on stage unless it's confirmed for that state.
- **Students carry it outward.** A student who presents to the township is doing the county agent's job.
- **The constraint is real.** Teachers have no time, no budget line and no specialist on staff. That is exactly the gap a partner can fill.

### 2.5 Where the incentive for gap 2 can come from

None of these is a market on its own. Stacked together, they can be one.

| Source | Mechanism | Durability |
|---|---|---|
| **Grants** (what funded this year) | One-time equipment and pilot money | Low. It ends when the grant ends. |
| **Civic output** | The class produces something the town needs: a runoff map, a tree-canopy survey, a roof inspection for the school itself. A township, conservation district or watershed group sponsors the question. | **Medium-high. This is the one to build.** It turns the project into a service with a local beneficiary. |
| **Workforce pipeline** | Local employers (surveying, utilities, ag, construction, public safety) fund equipment or mentor a class to grow future hires | Medium-high |
| **Community giving** | DonorsChoose, the education foundation, events like this one | Medium |
| **Industry in-kind** | Hardware, software seats, mentors from people in rooms like this | Depends on the asks being specific |

**Vision sentence:** *make the classroom produce something the community would have paid for, and the community starts paying for the classroom.*

### 2.6 Equivalents in other fields

Researched Oct 2, 2026. Dates are from well-documented histories, but re-check any line before it's said aloud.

| Field | The bridge | What it did | Youth as the channel? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Computing (UK) | **BBC Computer Literacy Project** (1980–89), **BBC Micro** (1981) | National TV series + a computer built to a BBC spec by Acorn + government part-funding for schools. **Detail and caveats in §2.7.** | Partly. Impact was mainly at home, not school. |
| Computing (US) | **MECC** (Minnesota, 1973) | State-funded shared computer access for schools, later Apple IIs and software (*The Oregon Trail*) | A generation's first computer was a school computer |
| Computing (again, deliberately) | **Raspberry Pi** (2012) → **BBC micro:bit** (2016) | Started because Cambridge saw computer-science applicants arriving with weaker skills. Explicitly modeled on the BBC Micro. | Yes. The micro:bit went free to UK 11–12-year-olds. |
| Internet | **E-Rate** (1996 Telecommunications Act) | Federal subsidy connecting schools and libraries to the internet | The school was often the town's first broadband |
| Manufacturing | **NIST Manufacturing Extension Partnership** (1988) | **Explicitly modeled on agricultural extension**: a center in every state bringing modern methods to small manufacturers | No. Adult to adult. |
| Hardware for non-engineers | **Arduino** (2005, Ivrea) | A microcontroller for design students. Cheap, hard to break, one USB cable. | Adopted by schools because it fit a class period |
| Robotics | **MIT Logo → LEGO Mindstorms (1998) → FIRST (1989)** | Learning research became a toy, then a league | **The competition is the county fair** |
| Engineering curriculum | **PSSC Physics** (MIT, 1956), **National Defense Education Act** (1958) | Researchers wrote the high-school course. Federal money bought lab equipment. | Gap 2 closed under deadline pressure (the space race) |
| Vocational / CTE | **Smith-Hughes Act** (1917) | Federal vocational education in high schools; ancestor of today's CTE funding | **Three years after Smith-Lever.** Washington built both bridges at once. |
| Electrification | **Rural Electrification Administration** (1935) | Power co-ops, plus traveling demonstration shows (the "REA circus") because farmers didn't know what electricity could do | A pure awareness fix |
| Household science | **Girls' tomato canning clubs** (~1910s, early extension) | Safe canning spread through teenage girls' clubs | Same mechanism as the corn clubs |
| Coastal / marine | **Sea Grant** (1966) | The land-grant model copied for coasts and fisheries | Proof the model transfers |

**The four ingredients.** Every case that worked had all four. Each failure was missing at least one.

1. **A device a beginner can't easily break.**
2. **A content layer that makes the lesson clear**, carried by a **supported teacher.**
3. **A publicly funded distribution channel.**
4. **A public showcase**: the county fair, a science fair, a FIRST match, the township meeting.

### 2.7 The deciding comparison: BBC Micro vs One Laptop per Child

| | **BBC Computer Literacy Project** (UK, 1980–89) | **One Laptop per Child** (2005 onward; Peru the largest rollout) |
|---|---|---|
| **Device** | BBC Micro, built by Acorn to a BBC spec: open, easy to get into, encouraging good programming habits | XO laptop designed for children, target price $100 |
| **Content** | TV series, books, courses, software. Programmes drew 0.5–1.2M viewers; the series reached **16% of UK adults** | Preinstalled software. Little lesson material for teachers |
| **Teacher support** | Schools in the subsidy scheme had to send a teacher to local in-service training. Quality varied widely by area | "Some training." Ten years on, teachers' digital skills hadn't improved and they rarely used the laptops in class |
| **Where it took hold** | **At home first.** Early buyers were mostly middle-class men under 45. Schools followed through a government scheme that part-funded **one** computer per school | Schools and homes. Kids used computers more and read fewer books |
| **Evidence quality** | **Weak.** One historical report and a self-selected survey of 292 people. The report asks itself whether it "would have happened anyway" | **Strong.** Randomized trial in 318 schools; 10-year follow-up in 531 schools |
| **Results** | Over 500k machines sold by 1986. Acorn started the ARM processor project. Many survey respondents went into software. **The gains slid back** once the push ended (Schmidt 2011, Royal Society 2012) | **No effect on math or language scores** at 15 months. After 10 years, no effect on school completion or university enrollment, some sign of *worse* grade progression. Computer skills did improve |

**Lesson for the talk:** the BBC succeeded through **public awareness** (TV, press, kids and parents excited together). Where it reached schools, results depended on whether a trained teacher was in the building. OLPC delivered devices without closing the teacher gap, and the best study in the field found no learning gains. **Same lesson twice: the device isn't the bottleneck. The teacher and the town are.**

**A direct precedent for the runoff map:** the **BBC Domesday Project** (1986) had **9,000 schoolchildren record information about their local area** for a national archive.

**The grant-design lesson (§5.3):** the UK's school scheme was criticised at the time "for only supporting the cost of the computer equipment, but not acknowledging the true cost of maintenance, software and staff training" (Blyth, 2012, p. 30).

### 2.8 Counterarguments the thesis has to survive

The original thesis ("gap 1 has a market; gap 2 has no market, it has a teacher") failed several of these. The revised §1 is written to survive them. Keep this table for Q&A; a robotics audience will raise some of these.

| # | Objection | How the revised thesis answers it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Markets don't close gap 1 alone.** The valley of death exists because early research doesn't pay. Much of it is public money (Mazzucato, *The Entrepreneurial State*, 2013). C-UAS itself is pulled by defense budgets. | Claim 1 now says "investors and public money." |
| 2 | **Gap 2 does have a market for consumer tech.** Smartphones, mobile money and DJI drones reached millions with no teacher. Iowa hybrid corn, the founding diffusion case, spread largely through salesmen and neighbors (Ryan & Gross, 1943; verify channel shares), and fastest where it was most profitable (Griliches, 1957). | Claim 2 narrows gap 2 to **capability and public-value uses**, and concedes markets deliver products. |
| 3 | **The model is linear; innovation often flows backwards.** Bush's 1945 linear model has been largely superseded (Kline & Rosenberg 1986; Stokes 1997). Users innovate (von Hippel 2005). **Betaflight, the firmware in our kit, came out of the hobbyist FPV community.** | Claim 3 makes the hobby (lateral) channel part of the argument instead of an exception to it. Q&A line: "Sometimes the community closes the gap itself. Schools are how more people get to be those hobbyists." |
| 4 | **Teachers aren't a free mechanism.** Extension worked with *paid, dedicated* county agents. The BBC's school results depended on uneven training. Peru's teachers didn't change practice. The World Bank's Training and Visit extension system proved too costly to sustain. | "If we back the teacher" is now in the thesis. See [`../sales/teacher-training.md`](../sales/teacher-training.md). |
| 5 | **Schools can widen the gap.** Well-resourced districts with grant writers adopt first. Technology amplifies existing capacity (Toyama, *Geek Heresy*, 2015). | Not fully answered. It's an argument for grant help and sponsor funding aimed at under-resourced schools (§5.3). Say so if asked. |
| 6 | **The student channel is weaker than 4-H's.** A corn-club kid's family ran the farm; a student's family doesn't run the township. A student map is educational, not decision-grade (legal survey work needs a licensed surveyor). | Don't say "a map that changes a decision" unless a professional partner validates it. Say "a map that starts a conversation." |
| 7 | **Awareness isn't the only barrier.** Procurement, liability, regulation and staff capacity matter too; you can only adopt what you have the knowledge to use (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990). | Claim 2 is about capability, which is absorptive capacity by another name. |
| 8 | **The speaker sells the prescribed solution.** | Disclosure stays in (§4). Say other routes (hobby clubs, makerspaces, other vendors) also work. |

### 2.9 Vertical vs lateral: why schools need a sideways channel

**The problem.** Institutions move knowledge vertically. In a company, a team solves a problem, it goes up the chain, and it comes back down to a different team later, or never. Teams beside each other rarely hear about it directly. A school is vertical too: teacher to student, class by class, school by school. Students in a class share less than they do around a game or a sport, because the class is assigned and the hobby is chosen.

**What spreads fast is lateral.** Sports, video games, music scenes and FPV flying spread peer to peer. Nobody assigns them. Someone shows a friend, the friend shows two more. That's how FPV produced its own firmware, racing leagues and parts market. The catch: lateral spread only runs inside the group already in the hobby.

**So the design goal:** use the school's reach (everyone, publicly funded, trusted) to **start** the hobby, then build lateral channels so it spreads like one.

| Lateral channel | What it looks like | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Competitions and leagues** | Inter-school build-and-fly challenges; existing drone racing leagues; events like the Action Space hackathon. The FIRST model: the match is the county fair. | Idea. Check which flight rules apply to club and competition flying. |
| **Students teaching students** | Second-year students run the first build sessions for the new cohort. Also eases the teacher bottleneck. | Idea. Fits the 3-year arc in [`../sales/money-model.md`](../sales/money-model.md). |
| **Clubs beyond the class** | An after-school club keeps it going when the unit ends | Idea |
| **Shared designs across schools** | A gallery where students publish frame designs and other schools print them | Idea, not a platform feature. Don't claim it. |
| **The simulator as a game** | Flight-sim practice is already game-shaped, and games spread laterally | Idea |
| **Teacher-to-teacher** | Teachers sharing fixes across schools (the BBC report's "two-way networks"); regional training days | See teacher-training §6 |
| **The showcase** | Students present to the town; parents and neighbors see it | §4 |

**Talk line (beat 6–8 or Q&A):**
> "Inside a company, a fix goes up the chain and back down before the team next door hears about it. School works the same way: top down. Hobbies don't. A kid learns a trick in a game and the whole lunch table has it by Friday. FPV drones spread that way, and their firmware came out of it. We want school to be where the hobby starts."

## 3. The tech side: what makes something classroom-ready

This is the part the hackathon room will respect most, because it's their weekend in a different shape. Their gap was **sim-to-real**. A school's gap is **lab-to-period**: something that works for an engineer on a Saturday has to work for 25 sixteen-year-olds in a 45-minute block, every day, with one teacher.

Source for everything below: the drone-building handbook ([`../../assets/courses/drone-building/outlines/drone-building-course-handbook-v3.4.txt`](../../assets/courses/drone-building/outlines/drone-building-course-handbook-v3.4.txt)). **It's a draft, not published course content.** Talk about it as "how we're designing it," not "what schools are using."

### 3.1 Design for manufacture by a classroom

| Decision | Why it matters for gap 2 |
|---|---|
| **Student-printed frame** (PLA prototypes, PETG final) | A crash costs a reprint, not an aircraft. Breaking parts stops being a budget problem and becomes the lesson. |
| **Stock-frame STL, plus print-and-ship** for schools without printers, with a makerspace as fallback | A school without a printer isn't locked out |
| **Two SKUs: solder and pre-soldered** | Many classrooms have no soldering lab. Pre-soldering removes the biggest safety and skill barrier while keeping the rest of the build. ("Does the school have a soldering lab?" is now a sales qualification question.) |
| **Solder joints counted on the BOM** (16 + 4 on the solder SKU, 0 for students on pre-soldered) | Assembly time and failure points are designed numbers, not surprises |
| **Two kit tiers**: Base (design the whole printed frame), Video (printed parts on a stock frame) | Lets a school short on time still do real design work |
| **Per-aircraft cost** ~$275 Base / ~$465 Video (list, from the draft parts list) | **Don't say this on stage until pricing is confirmed.** If it's confirmed, it's a strong line: an autonomous-capable aircraft for the price of a textbook set. |

### 3.2 Design for the 45-minute period

The invisible work: most of the engineering in a lesson is **removing the ways a class period gets lost.**

- **Sequencing is a design decision.** Physics comes right before Design so students budget weight and choose materials *before* they draw a frame. The order teaches the engineering process.
- **Safety gates are hard gates.** Props stay off until the bench test. No motor spins until the failsafe has been demonstrated with props off. These are checkpoints in the curriculum, not posters on the wall.
- **Pre-empt the session-killers.** One dead radio at bind time can burn a whole session. The teacher can hand out a settings backup when time is short. Batteries per period are a budget line, which is exactly why "two drones for a class of four teams" matters.
- **Failure is scheduled.** Every test flight ends with *what the failure taught → a CAD or software iteration.* That's the hackathon loop, slowed down to fit a semester.
- **The law is a unit.** A custom build has its own regulatory questions (registration, Remote ID / broadcast module, Part 107 vs recreational). Teaching it is part of closing the expertise cliff.

**Line for the room:** *"You know the hard part isn't the model, it's the hardware being in the room and working. In a classroom the hard part is the hardware working at 10:05 for twenty-five people, and again at 10:50 for the next twenty-five."*

### 3.3 What engineers can do about it, concretely

These are the requests that convert a sympathetic room into help:

1. **Write one README for a 16-year-old.** Classroom-grade docs on an open stack are worth more than a donation.
2. **Design for the second gap.** Make it repairable, printable, sub-$X, with a safe default config and a failsafe that's on out of the box.
3. **Adopt a classroom.** One mentor visit a semester, or judge a student showcase.
4. **Hardware in-kind.** Airframes, batteries, a printer. (This ties to the ask: keynote doc Path C.)
5. **Bring a real question.** If your company or town has a mapping, inspection or sensing problem, give it to a class as a project.

## 4. How we work with schools (the model, stated plainly)

Keep this short on stage. It's mostly for Q&A and follow-up conversations.

```
 Teacher says yes ──► Drone Edge: curriculum, kit design, Part 107 prep, teacher support
        │
        ▼
 Community partner brings a real question (township, conservation district, employer)
        │
        ▼
 Students fly, build, model, and present the answer publicly (showcase / board / township meeting)
        │
        ▼
 Community sees it ──► awareness ──► local funding + next year's partners
```

- **The school owns the program and the data.** Drone Edge is a vendor and a partner, not the owner.
- **Funding is diversified on purpose** (§2.5, §5.3) so the program outlives any single grant.
- **The showcase is the awareness engine.** Plan it from day one and invite the people who'd fund year two.
- **Disclosure stays in.** We sell curriculum to schools. Anything raised in our presence buys third-party hardware, and the district does the purchasing (keynote §3.5).

## 5. What this means for Drone Edge

Internal. This section is for us, not the stage. It maps the research onto the company's vision ([`../sales/positioning.md`](../sales/positioning.md)) and onto how we help schools get funded ([`/schools/funding`](../../drone/src/app/schools/funding/page.tsx), [`../sales/money-model.md`](../sales/money-model.md) §5.1).

### 5.1 Where Drone Edge sits

The positioning vision says we help programs "move from curiosity to credible FAA Part 107 preparation and applied drone skills." In the language of this doc: **Drone Edge is in the business of closing gap 2 for drones, through schools.** That's a sharper reason to exist than "a course platform." It also tells us what we can't skip.

In the producer/promoter terms of §1 claim 4: **Drone Edge is the producer.** We take what engineers and the FPV community built and package it into a kit, a lesson and a teacher who can run it. The school and teacher are the venue and promoter. Our job is to make them good at it, and to build the lateral channels (§2.9) so the hobby spreads beyond the class.

### 5.2 The four ingredients, scored honestly

| Ingredient | What Drone Edge has today | Status | The lesson from §2.7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Device** | Build kits, printable frame, pre-soldered option | **Draft.** Kits quoted separately; build course not live (positioning § Prohibited) | Necessary but never sufficient. OLPC had a great device. |
| **2. Content** | Part 107 course: structured units, exams, progress | **Live** | The BBC's content (TV, books) is what made the device matter |
| **2b. Teacher support** | Org manager dashboard, assignments, per-student progress | **Partly live.** The *tooling* exists; a defined **teacher onboarding / PD offering does not** | **This is the bottleneck in both case studies.** It's the piece we most need to design deliberately. |
| **3. Distribution channel** | Schools (CTE / STEM), funded by grants and sponsors | Live as a sales motion | Schools are the channel. The BBC's school results varied with local training capacity. |
| **4. Showcase** | Civic projects (the runoff map), events, case studies with permission | **Aspirational.** First project is in progress at one school | The BBC's real engine was public awareness. Domesday shows a kid-built local survey can be the showcase. |

**Strategic takeaway:** the place to win is **2b**, not the kit. Anyone can sell drones to schools. Few vendors make the teacher successful at 10:05 on a Tuesday. Treat teacher support as a product, not a courtesy:

- A teacher onboarding path before students start (Part 107 for the teacher, a dry-run build, the safety gates).
- Session-saving material: settings backups, a "what to do when a radio won't bind" card, battery plans per period.
- A named person to call during the first semester. Ames's Paraguay finding (only schools with paid local trainers succeeded, and the money ran out) says **this support must be in the recurring price, not funded by a one-off grant.**
- The dashboard as the teacher's evidence for their own administration and funders.

These are product decisions to scope, not claims to make. Don't advertise teacher PD until it exists. Design draft: [`../sales/teacher-training.md`](../sales/teacher-training.md).

### 5.3 How we hope to bring grants to schools

**What we are and aren't.** We don't administer grants and can't guarantee eligibility or awards ([`/schools/funding`](../../drone/src/app/schools/funding/page.tsx) disclaimer; positioning § Prohibited). Our role is to help a school **tell an accurate, fundable story** and to **design programs that survive after the grant ends.**

**Principle 1: fund the whole cost, not the box.** The UK school scheme was criticised for paying for equipment but not "maintenance, software and staff training." Chichester's grant bought two drones when the teacher asked for four. Help schools budget the full cost of ownership in the application:

| Budget line | Why it's in the grant |
|---|---|
| Aircraft / kits | The visible part |
| Spares, props, **batteries per period** | What makes a 45-minute class work |
| Course seats (Part 107 + build) | The content layer |
| **Teacher onboarding / PD** | The bottleneck (§5.2). Many RFAs pair "equipment plus professional development," per the funding page |
| Year-2 consumables | So year 2 doesn't stall |
| Evaluation | Funders ask for it (Principle 3) |

**Principle 2: grants start programs; local value keeps them going.** Plan the funding ladder at the start, not when the grant runs out:

```
 Year 1   Seed grant ─── PA SMART (PA), Perkins V, Title IV where allowed, competitive state/federal STEM-CTE
             │
 Year 1–2 Civic sponsor ─ township / conservation district / watershed group funds the question the class answers
             │
 Year 2   Sponsor-a-classroom ─ local employer (money-model §5.1); in-kind hardware
             │
 Ongoing  Community giving ─ DonorsChoose, education foundation, events
             │
 Year 3+  District budget line ─ the program has earned a place in the CTE budget
```

The BBC's gains slid back when the national push stopped. A program funded only by year-1 grant money will do the same. The money model's 3-year arc and multi-year sponsor naming exist for this reason.

**Principle 3: measure from day one, claim nothing early.** The BBC story rests on weak evidence; OLPC was measured rigorously and found wanting. Funders increasingly ask which one you are. Build evaluation into the grant:
- Real dashboard numbers only: students enrolled, units completed, exam scores, aircraft built.
- Civic deliverables: the map, the report, the township presentation.
- Part 107 outcomes only as **school-reported**, never as a Drone Edge pass-rate claim.
- If a school wants a stronger design (a comparison group, a pre/post), help them set it up. It makes year-2 grants easier.

**Principle 4: the conflict line, again.** We sell to the schools we help get funded. Keep it clean:
- We help with **narrative and program design**. The district writes and submits its own application and does its own procurement.
- **Federal funds:** 2 CFR 200.319(b) excludes contractors that "develop or draft specifications, requirements, statements of work, or invitations for bids" from competing for that procurement. Helping a school describe its program is different from drafting the spec it then buys against. **Confirm where that line sits with counsel before doing grant work on a federal-funds deal.**
- Fundraising events stay third-party-hardware-only (keynote §3.5).

**Grant story in one paragraph** (for a school's narrative, adapted to the RFA's exact terms):
> Students earn a federal credential (the FAA Remote Pilot Certificate) and design, build and fly an aircraft, then use it to answer a real question for their community, such as mapping where the township's stormwater goes. The budget covers equipment and the training and consumables that make it work in a class period, and the program is structured to continue with local partners after the grant.

## 6. Talk shape: 15–20 minutes

Built as roughly **8 minutes of talk and 8–10 minutes of Q&A**, with the ask at the end. If Q&A is dead, use the seeded questions in §7.

| Min | Beat | Core line / content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1.5 | **Open on their weekend** | Reuse the reward-function cold open from keynote §6.1. "Perceive, decide, act" is the whole job. |
| 1.5–3.5 | **Gap 1** | "Everything in this room crossed a gap to get here: a paper became a stack became a Tello you could fly on Sunday. That gap has money pointed at it. This weekend's theme exists because someone will buy it." |
| 3.5–6 | **Gap 2 + awareness** | "Here's the gap with nobody pointed at it." A sub-$300 aircraft can survey a watershed, and the township down the road doesn't know that. "You can't demand what you don't know exists." |
| 6–8 | **It's been tried before** | Extension and 4-H corn clubs: the kid as the awareness channel. Then the BBC Micro vs OLPC line (below). "We don't have a county agent for robotics. We have a high school in every county." Close the beat with the vertical-vs-lateral line (§2.9). |
| 8–10 | **The school example** | Chichester: the runoff grant, students handing a township a map. The tech-side work from §3 (printable frames, pre-soldered SKU, failsafe gates, the 45-minute period). "Our sim-to-real is lab-to-period." |
| 10–11 | **The model + disclosure** | §4 in two sentences. Disclose the vendor relationship **here**, before anything resembling an ask. |
| 11–18 | **Q&A** | Seeded questions in §7 if the room is quiet; answers in §8. |
| 18–20 | **The ask + close** | Five specific asks (§3.3) + the hardware gap ("two drones, teacher asked for four") via the charity QR from the keynote doc. Close: *"The equipment has to be there on the day they get curious."* |

**The history line (beat 6–8):**
> "Britain's BBC Micro is remembered as a school computer. The history of it says the real impact came through TV and at home, and in schools it depended on whether anyone trained the teacher. Peru gave every kid a laptop, ran the best study anyone has, and ten years later found no gain in reading or math. Same lesson twice: the device isn't the bottleneck. The teacher and the town are."

**If the slot shrinks to 5 minutes,** keep the opening, the gap 2 beat, the school example and the close, and use keynote §6.1.

## 7. Questions to put to the room

Use these to start discussion or turn the talk into a conversation. Pick 2–3.

1. *"Hands up if the first time you flew, built or coded something was because the equipment happened to be in a room you were already in."* (This warms the room and sets up the close.)
2. *"Who here knows what their own town spends on stormwater surveys?"* (Almost nobody will. That's the awareness gap, demonstrated live.)
3. *"What's the cheapest thing in your stack that you wish every high school had?"*
4. *"If you open-sourced your project this weekend, could a teacher run it without you in the room? What would it take?"*
5. *"Whose job is it to close the second gap: companies, government, schools, or people in this room?"*
6. *"What would make your company pay for a classroom's output instead of donating to it?"*
