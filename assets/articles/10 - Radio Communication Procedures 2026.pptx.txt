RADIO COMMUNICATION PROCEDURES

RADIO COMMUNICATION IN THE NAS
Radio communication is critical for safe aircraft operations in the National Airspace System (NAS).
Pilots use radios to: 
Give and receive information before, during, and after flight
Communicate safety issues such as unexpected weather or inflight emergencies


UNDERSTANDING PROPER RADIO PROCEDURES
Proper radio phraseology improves safety and efficiency.
The AIM Pilot/Controller Glossary provides: 
Standard terminology & examples of proper radio transmissions
The phonetic alphabet is used for aircraft identification (e.g., “Alpha, Bravo, Charlie”).
Do not make up your own words to denote the letters
Knowing this helps remote pilots understand manned-aircraft communications around them.
Monitoring radio frequencies of local tower maybe a  requirement of airspace authorization

COMMON TRAFFIC ADVISORY FREQUENCY (CTAF)
CTAF - Common Traffic Advisory Frequency
Used to conduct airport advisory practices at non‑towered airport. – Monitor if working near non-towered airport
CTAF may be: 
UNICOM
MULTICOM
FSS
Tower frequency (when Tower is closed)

RECOMMENDED TRAFFIC ADVISORY PRACTICES FOR REMOTE PILOTS

RECOMMENDED COMMUNICATIONS PRACTICES
When flying within 5 miles of a non-towered airport check appropriate frequencies on the Chart Supplement and sectional charts for the area.
Pilots must be alert and look for other traffic when operating near an airport without an operating control tower 
CTAF is a radio frequency used for making airport advisory communications.
It may be provided by UNICOM, MULTICOM, a Flight Service Station (FSS), or a control tower frequency.



RADIO COMMUNICATIONS
UNICOM – non-government radio station used at airports without a control tower or FSS or a full-time or part-time UNICOM station 
122.8 is the frequency
Gives airport advisory information when pilots request it including:
Runway in use, local weather observations, airport conditions
UNICOM advisories are not ATC instructions - pilots still self‑announce and maintain traffic awareness.

RADIO COMMUNICATIONS
At non-towered airports without a UNICOM, or any other listed frequency, pilots should use the MULTICOM frequency of 122.9 to self-announce
At airports lacking air traffic control, pilots use the MULTICOM frequency as a CTAF to self-announce their intentions.


RADIO COMMUNICATIONS
Flight Service Station (FSS) - an air traffic facility proving pilots with preflight and in-flight services, such as weather briefings, flight plan processing, search and rescue initiation, and NOTAM updates. 
Unlike Air Traffic Control (ATC), FSS does not issue clearances

RADIO COMMUNICATIONS
U.S. aircraft have unique “N” registration numbers (e.g., N123AB)
Pronounced using the phonetic alphabet (e.g., November One-Two-Three-Alpha-Bravo)
Light GA aircraft: use manufacturer name
Example: Cessna 172 → “Cessna One-Two-Three-Alpha-Bravo”


RADIO COMMUNICATIONS
Larger aircraft (turbo-prop/jet): use model name
Example: Citation → “Citation One-Two-Three-Alpha-Bravo”
Airliners: use company name + flight number
Example: Southwest 711 → “Southwest Seven-One-One” 
Some airlines use special call signs 
Example: British Airways → “Speedbird”
