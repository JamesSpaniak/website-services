# Drone building — classroom soldering lab: cost, safety, options

**Status:** Research note, Sep 9 2026. Answers the question "if a school has to buy soldering equipment for this class, what does it cost and is it safe?" and lays out the kit options that follow from the answer. Prices are US list/street prices on the date checked, pre-tax and shipping. Items marked *verify* are estimates or unconfirmed. Joint counts and the Unit 6 step list come from [`parts-list-draft-v2.md`](parts-list-draft-v2.md) §3 and §6 and the [course outline v3.2](../outlines/drone-building-course-outline-v3.md); this file does not change either. The option-B labor derivation in §5 is the cost basis the parts list and outline quote for the Pre-soldered SKU.

**Frame of reference.** The Base and Video kits each need **16 solder joints per aircraft** (12 motor leads to ESC pads, 2 battery pigtail, 2 capacitor), all on the 4-in-1 ESC of the Flywoo GOKU F722 Pro Mini V2 45A 20×20 stack. Motor leads are 24 AWG × 150 mm (XILO 1404); the pigtail is XT60; pads sit on a heavy copper plane. The receiver adds **0–4 joints** depending on the part: the BetaFPV ELRS Nano ships pads and loose 30 AWG wires (4 fine-wire joints at the RX end, plug at the FC), a plug-terminated receiver adds none. Nothing else is soldered: FC↔ESC is an 8-pin cable, O4 is a plug, and the BeeID GPS + Remote ID module (one per aircraft) plugs into the FC's 4-pin UART5 socket.

## 1. What a classroom soldering station needs

**Iron.** Temperature-controlled, closed-loop, 60–100 W class so the tip recovers on the ESC power pads. Set 350–380 °C for SAC305 on 24 AWG and motor pads, 400 °C for the XT60 pigtail (14–16 AWG leads into the battery pads — the largest thermal mass in the kit; pre-tin both, hold the iron longer rather than turning it up further). Un-regulated "power-dial" irons (Weller WLC100 class) work but overshoot and eat tips on lead-free; acceptable if already owned. A **2.4 mm chisel** (Hakko T18-D24 / Weller ETB / Pinecil D24 / T12-D24) is the one tip for every joint in this kit.

**Solder.** Recommend **lead-free SAC305 (Sn96.5 Ag3.0 Cu0.5), 0.8 mm, no-clean rosin core**. Melts 217–220 °C vs 183 °C for 63/37; it is less forgiving and needs the hotter, better-regulated iron above, but it removes the lead-ingestion hazard, matches FIRST, RPI and Pennsylvania's STEELS guidance, and avoids leaded-waste handling (tips, sponges, clippings become hazardous waste under university EHS rules). 63/37 is easier for first joints and cheaper per pound; only use it where the school already stocks it and enforces hand-washing and no food. Do not mix alloys on one tip. A **no-clean flux pen** is mandatory with lead-free: flux the pad and the wire before tinning; most "cold" student joints are a flux problem.

**Fume extraction: bench-top vs arm.** Bench-top carbon-filter fans remove odour and most flux aerosol when the intake is within 15 cm of the joint; they are what FIRST and university EHS pages mean by "local exhaust." Do not rely on room ventilation alone.

## 2. Class sizing

Students work in pairs on one aircraft; each student solders 8 of the 16 joints. Only one pair can use a station at a time, so stations, not kits, set the pace.

| Item | 10 kits / 20 students |
|------|----------------------|
| Stations | **5 student stations + 1 teacher/demo station = 6.** 10 pairs rotate through 5 stations, two rounds. Non-soldering pairs do the mechanical parts of Unit 6 steps 2–4 (motors bolted to arms, FC on the stack, receiver and BeeID seated and plugged) or Unit 5 print checks in the same session |
| Iron time per aircraft | 45–60 min first-time (see §4) → 10 pairs × ~50 min = ~500 station-minutes ÷ 5 stations ≈ **100 min ≈ two 50-min sessions** for all 16 joints on every aircraft |
| Class cost, budget tier | 6 × ~$185 ≈ **$1,100** + shared ≈ **$1,200** |
| Class cost, standard tier | 6 × ~$315 ≈ **$1,900** + shared ≈ **$2,000** |
| Class cost, "already have it" | 6 tips + 6 flux pens + brass cleaners + solder ≈ **$150–300** |

**Shared items (once per class).** Solder: 160–200 kit joints + practice ≈ 100–150 g per semester *verify*; a **1 lb spool lasts 2–3 semesters**. Kester 275 SAC305 0.8 mm 1 lb **$121–174** ([Concorde](https://concorde-electronics.com/shop/kester-24-7068-7618-sac-305-lead-free-wire-solder-sn96-5ag3-0cu0-5/), [Hisco](https://www.hisco.com/Product/24-7068-7601-16600)); import-brand SAC305 100 g spools **$15–25** *verify*; Kester 44 63/37 1 lb **$37.95** ([Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004X4L6QG)) if leaded is already policy. Burn kit: burn-gel packets **$4.08/6** ([Imperial](https://www.imperialsupplies.com/item/9204561)) plus the room's first-aid kit and a cold tap. Practice stock: scrap 24 AWG wire and a strip of copper-clad or perfboard, ~$10.

**Consumables per semester.** Tips: lead-free at 380 °C wears a chisel in roughly a school year of class use → 1 tip/station/year ($6.50–8). Filters: replace each semester or when the flux smell passes through — Hakko A1001 5-pack **$25.47**, Weller WSA350F 3-pack **$20.21**, KOTTO ~$13/pack *verify*. Flux pens: one per station per year. Budget **~$100–150/semester** for a 6-station class.

## 3. Safety

**Actual hazards, in order of frequency.**

| Hazard | What actually happens | Mitigation |
|--------|----------------------|------------|
| Burns | Tip at 350–400 °C; most incidents are grabbing the wrong end, brushing the barrel, or a hot component/wire (RPI, TN Tech). Minor first-degree burns are the routine injury | Iron lives in its stand, never on the mat; tweezers hold wires; cotton sleeves; hot-work zone marked on the mat; cold water 15 min, cover, no ointment (TN Tech); report per school procedure |
| Flux fumes | The visible smoke is **rosin (colophony) pyrolysis products** — aldehydes, measured as formaldehyde. Irritant and a respiratory **sensitizer** (occupational asthma). NIOSH REL 0.1 mg/m³ 10-h TWA; OSHA has no PEL; ACGIH: "reduce to as low as possible" (NIOSH HETA 96-0016; NIOSH PEL88) | Bench-top extractor at each station, head to the side not above the work (MIT), room ventilation, short heat times; asthmatic students told in advance and seated by the extractor or excused from the iron |
| Lead | **Not a vapour hazard at iron temperatures**: lead boils at 1,740 °C and its vapour pressure at 350 °C is negligible; LBNL and university EHS state lead fume is not generated in electronic soldering. Exposure route is **hand-to-mouth ingestion** from contaminated hands, bench, and clipped ends (UNC, CMU) | Lead-free SAC305 removes the hazard. If leaded: wash hands with soap after every session, wipe bench, no food/drink/gum/nail-biting at the bench, leaded waste in a labelled container |
| Eye | Solder "spits" when flux boils; clipped wire ends fly (PA STEELS) | ANSI Z87.1 glasses on for everyone in the hot-work zone, including spectators; goggles over prescription glasses (FIRST) |
| Fire | Iron left on paper, cardboard, or a LiPo; damaged cord | Silicone mat, no paper at the bench, **no battery in the room during soldering**, capacitor discharged, extinguisher location known; auto-standby stations (FX-888DX, WE1010, Pinecil) sleep when idle |
| Electrical | Frayed cords, soldering a live circuit | Inspect cords each session; nothing powered while soldering (PA STEELS) |

**Supervision.** No published student:iron ratio was found. FIRST requires adults present at all soldering; a maker-education program (Eurekus) runs 2–4 irons per adult; university shops require trained supervision. Working rule for this course: **one adult with line of sight to every hot iron, ≤ 6 irons per adult**, and no iron powered until the Unit 1 shop cert is signed. *verify* against the district's CTE lab policy.

**What published guidance says.**

| Source | Position |
|--------|----------|
| FIRST Robotics safety manual (current) §3.7 | Lead-free "highly recommended"; electric irons only; eye and face protection; well-ventilated area; cotton clothing covering arms and legs; no food or drink in the soldering area; wash hands; fire-resistant surface; iron in holder; dispose of solder waste as hazardous even if lead-free |
| NSTA — STEM course safety protocols for science teachers assigned to T&E | Teachers need tool-specific training before soldering irons; hazard analysis and risk assessment per activity; SDS on file; **ANSI/ISEA Z87.1 D3** eye protection for soldering; heat-resistant gloves for hot tools; aprons; ventilation for soldering. NSTA's national STEM lab study flagged "ventilation for soldering" as a common facility gap |
| NSTA — Liability of science educators | Duty of care; "reasonably prudent person" standard; document training and maintain engineering controls (ventilation) |
| Pennsylvania STEELS Safety Guide §3.4.1.2.5.1 (state-level) | Lead-free whenever possible; solder under a dedicated hood or next to a portable extractor; Z87.1 glasses; no live circuits; no food; return iron to stand; wipe bench; wash hands |
| OSHA / NIOSH | Lead standard 29 CFR 1910.1025 (PEL 50 µg/m³, action level 30 µg/m³) applies to employers; soldering at iron temperatures does not generate lead fume (LBNL). Rosin-core pyrolysis products: NIOSH REL 0.1 mg/m³ as formaldehyde; no OSHA PEL |
| University EHS (UNC, MIT, CMU, RPI, Tennessee Tech) | Substitute lead-free; local exhaust or bench extractor; wash hands and arms; RPI prohibits leaded solder outright; MIT: keep head to the side of the work |
| District policy examples | Wichita USD 259 P6901: written safety test per piece of CTE equipment, retested annually. Altona MS (St. Vrain, CO): middle-school "Solder Certified" course before students may use its 4 stations. Calgary Board of Education AR3052 and Halton DSB (Canada): documented training/"safety passport" before any tool; parents may request in writing that a child's tool use be restricted |

**Age.** Soldering is routine in **high-school CTE and robotics** and common in **middle-school makerspaces with a certification step** (Altona MS is a public example); maker programs solder with younger children by having the adult hold the iron. No US district was found that bans soldering; the pattern is *train → test → certify → supervise*. For this course (high school, CTE), the Unit 1 shop cert is the certification step.

**Insurance / liability.** Nothing soldering-specific was found. Districts' general liability covers negligence claims; student accident insurance pays medical on a no-fault basis. What lowers exposure (NSTA, PA STEELS): documented safety instruction with dates and names, the signed shop cert, SDS for solder and flux on file with the nurse and CTE office, a written hazard analysis, and functioning extraction. Give the CTE director those four documents with the kit; *verify* with the district risk manager whether the class needs a separate hot-work notification.

## 4. Time cost

| Step | First-time student | Notes |
|------|-------------------|-------|
| One joint, all-in (cut, strip, flux, tin wire, tin pad, join, hold, inspect) | **2–5 min** | Contact time is 1–3 s; the rest is prep and cooling. FPV guides agree most beginners make reliable joints "within an hour" |
| 16 joints, one aircraft, one pair | **35–80 min; plan 45–60** | 8 joints per student; the pigtail and capacitor take longer than motor leads (heavy pads) |
| Practice on scrap before the aircraft | 10–15 min per student | Cheapest way to avoid a lifted pad on a $91 stack |
| Teacher demo (Unit 6 opener) | 10–15 min | Live joint under a document camera or the *step clip*; iron temperature, flux, tin-tin-join, inspection |
| Rework | 5–10 min per bad joint | Expect 1–2 per class of 10 aircraft |

Net: soldering adds **about one and a half to two 50-min sessions** to Unit 6 for a 10-aircraft class on 5 stations, plus the Unit 1 tools/PPE material that already exists. If the receiver is the pad-type BetaFPV Nano, add 4 fine-wire (30 AWG) joints and ~10 min per aircraft; a plug-terminated receiver adds nothing. The BeeID and O4 are plugs and add no iron time.

## 5. Options for our kit

Our labor for option B: 16 joints ≈ 15–20 min for a practiced builder + ~5 min QC (continuity, polarity, motor order/direction spin-up, pull test, photo) ≈ **25 min per kit** (+~5 min if we also terminate a pad-type receiver); at $30–40/h loaded ≈ **$12–17 labor + ~$1 materials**, so **+$15–20 per kit at cost**. Priced at **+$25–40 per kit** — the low end covers labor at margin, the high end covers the receiver termination, the QC spin-up test, and a rework allowance. This is the figure the parts list v2 §3 and outline §2 use. *verify* against Joe's actual build time.

**Reading.** A is right for CTE labs that already own irons (most do, per parts list §2). B is the product that opens general STEM classrooms and is what the no-solder competitors sell; it costs us ~$15–20 a kit and no design constraint (the 150 mm leads outreach any 3.5" arm). D is the bridge for a school with one iron and one willing teacher. C is not a real option. A no-solder *ESC with motor plugs* would remove the joints entirely, but Joe's Sep 9 finding is that plug-and-play boards exist only for ≤ 2" drones, so it is not available in this size class; B is the route to a no-solder kit.

## 6. Advanced track / "course 2" — *proposal, not locked*

A second course, **Drone Repair and Custom Builds**, for students who finished the building course or arrive as enthusiasts, offered as a semester-2 elective or a club block. Soldering is the point, not a hurdle: the course opens with a soldering unit (tin-tin-join, thermal mass, lead-free technique, desoldering with wick and pump, inspection to a simple IPC-style pass/fail rubric), then works through the repairs a semester of flying generates on the Base/Video fleet — **motor swap** (3 joints), **ESC swap** (16 joints) or **FC swap** (plugs only, plus reflash and settings restore), **pigtail and capacitor replacement**, **custom harnesses** (compass module on the Video kit, LED strips, buzzer, a plug-terminated receiver, a printed-frame wiring plan), and finally a **scratch build** from a parts list the student writes and prices themselves. Every unit ends on the Unit 8 bench gates (smoke stopper, motor direction, failsafe) so repaired aircraft re-enter the flying pool.

What it asks of the school: a working soldering lab at the standard tier (§1) with extraction, a parts budget for spares and one or two scratch builds (~$200–400 per class), and a teacher who has completed the building course's shop cert plus our soldering module. It relates to the kits directly: it consumes the crash spares the parts list already budgets, keeps the school's fleet alive past year one without buying new kits, and is the natural home for the "16 joints" if v1 moves to pre-soldered looms. Sales framing: course 1 builds and flies; course 2 keeps them flying and lets students design their own. Price, length, and whether it needs its own kit are open.
